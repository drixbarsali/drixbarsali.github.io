### Session Deep Dive: What Pirate Crews Teach Modern Tech Teams

#### Yohooo! – The art of being less of a developer and more of a pirate 🏴‍☠️

What can pirates teach us about teamwork in software development? Much more than Hollywood clichés suggest. In this talk at GDG Palermo, we look past cinematic myths to examine how real pirate organizations operated: decentralized networks built on shared rules, mutual aid, transparent governance, and collective accountability.

Why does this matter for software engineers, Tech Leads, and Engineering Managers today? Because most software projects do not fail due to a lack of raw coding talent or the wrong JavaScript framework. They stall when communication breaks down, when developers work in isolated silos, and when **ego** prevents people from asking questions or admitting mistakes early.

### Fundamental Themes Explored on Stage

#### 1. ⚓ Psychological Safety in Everyday Engineering
Creating an environment where asking for help is never seen as a weakness is the foundation of sustainable velocity. In many workplaces, developers spend hours—or days—stuck on a problem in silence because they worry that asking a question in the team channel will make them look less competent.

During the session, we break down actionable ways Tech Leads and Senior Developers can model vulnerability:
- **Publicly asking questions**: When senior engineers ask for clarification on business logic or architectural edge cases, it signals to the entire team that curiosity is expected.
- **Blameless incident response**: Treating bugs in staging or production as failures of the system and test suite rather than failures of an individual developer.
- **Constructive code reviews**: Phrasing pull request feedback as collaborative questions (*"What do you think about extracting this into a pure pipe to avoid unnecessary change detection cycles?"*) rather than blunt commands.

#### 2. ⚓ Rethinking Junior and Senior Dynamics
Software teams often suffer from a double illusion: juniors believe they have nothing valuable to contribute to architectural discussions, while seniors believe they must always have an immediate, flawless answer to every problem.

On a pirate vessel, every crew member had a voice in the ship's council, regardless of how recently they had joined. In software engineering, fresh eyes often spot over-engineered abstractions or missing documentation that veterans have grown blind to. Pairing juniors and seniors as equals on complex features accelerates onboarding, prevents knowledge silos (reducing the team's "bus factor"), and keeps senior engineers grounded.

#### 3. ⚓ Taming the Ego: Commits vs. Crew Outcomes
A team's real value is never measured by an individual's commit graph or lines of code written in isolation—it is measured by the crew's ability to navigate complexity and deliver reliable software together.

When we shift our definition of "seniority" from *how complex the code you write is* to *how effectively you unblock and empower the people around you*, the entire engineering culture transforms:
- Codebases become simpler, more readable, and easier to test.
- Knowledge is shared through documentation and pair programming rather than hoarded for job security.
- Sprints become predictable because nobody hides blockers out of pride.
