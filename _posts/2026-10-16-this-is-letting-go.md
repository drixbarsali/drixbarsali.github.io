It is a luggage shaped by over twenty years of gigs, tours, and real life.

> «Go on alone  
> 'Cause I won't follow  
> But this isn't giving up  
> No, this is letting go»

Sometimes we face problems that feel never-ending. Situations that linger day after day, wearing us down, making us uncomfortable, heavy like a boulder. We end up feeling guilty, thinking it must be our fault if we can't move forward or resolve them the right way.

But the truth is different: there comes a moment when we have genuinely done everything within our power. And understanding when to stop does not mean giving up—it simply means letting go.

### Working with People

When working with people, we frequently face relational friction and difficulties that feel insurmountable:

- **Tension dynamics** that keep resurfacing, eroding the harmony of the team.
- **The frustration** of trying every possible way to help someone or align on a vision, only to feel a brick wall on the other side.
- **That heavy guilt** that makes you think: *"If this dynamic isn't working, it's because I, as a leader or teammate, am not doing enough"*.

You end up overburdening yourself, believing it is entirely up to you to fix every friction, every bad mood, every incompatibility.

Yet reality is different: there comes a point where you have given all you could give. You listened, you established a safe environment, you sought dialogue. If the situation doesn't shift, it is not your personal failure: there are contexts, attitudes, or incompatibilities that you simply cannot force on your own.

### Giving Up vs. Letting Go

There is a huge distinction between giving up and letting go:

- **Giving up** means apathy and washing your hands of it from the very beginning.
- **Letting go** means having the clarity to recognize the boundary between what you can influence and what is beyond your control. It means stopping guilt from draining you, to protect your own energy and, above all, the well-being of the whole team.

A great leader is not someone who stubbornly fights one-sided battles until burnout. It is someone who knows when to step back, accept reality as it is, and choose where to truly invest care and attention.

It is not surrender. It is respect for oneself and for the peace of the team.
